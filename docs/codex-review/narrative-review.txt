**1. What It Still Feels Like**

It still smells like a framework wrapper: “I uploaded guru content, split it into agents, and made it sound strategic.”

The weak familiar parts:

- “7-agent workforce” risks sounding inflated. If only one specialist runs after triage, reviewers may see a router plus prompt templates.
- Hormozi branding can feel like borrowed authority.
- 5 eval cases is tiny. It proves you tuned for five examples, not that the system is robust.
- LLM-judge scoring is weak proof unless you show the actual rubric and failure modes.

What feels different:

- The refusal-to-answer hook is real. “User asks pricing/sales, system diagnoses upstream offer” is more memorable than another assistant giving advice.
- The v1/v2/v3 regression story is useful if framed as eval hardening, not prompt fiddling.

**2. Biggest Claim-Proof Gap**

Claim: “diagnostic-first GTM system.”

Proof: “It routed 5 canned cases correctly.”

A real GTM operator will distrust:

- No evidence the diagnosis is correct in the real world.
- No uncertainty handling. The demo input could be Offer, Sales, ICP, urgency, champion quality, procurement friction, or pipeline source quality.
- “Perceived Likelihood gap” sounds plausible, but the system needs to show why it ruled out bad sales process, wrong ICP, weak economic buyer access, or non-urgent pain.
- 88% on 25 LLM-judged rules is not strong. It is bootcamp-valid, not operator-valid.

The missing proof is not more agents. It is a visible diagnostic audit trail: “I considered Sales/Pricing/Leads/Retention, rejected them because X, routed Offer because Y.”

**3. Loom Narrative**

The arc is mostly right, but the attention risk is architecture/explaining too early.

Open with the output, not the system.

Best sequence:

1. Paste the “sales/pricing problem.”
2. Show Triage refusing the surface answer.
3. Freeze on the exact line: “Do not cut price yet. This is an Offer / Perceived Likelihood problem.”
4. Show the rejected paths: why not Sales, why not Pricing, why not Leads.
5. Show the specialist’s one-week action plan.
6. Then evals.
7. Then architecture.

The sit-up moment: not “it routes to Offer.” The sit-up moment is:  
“Dropping price will probably make this worse because the buyer doesn’t believe the outcome enough yet; reduce perceived risk before reducing price.”

That is sharper than “risk reversal recommended.”

**4. Eval-Driven Iteration**

Do not frame it as “v1 was bad, v2 was also bad, v3 works.”

Frame it as:

“I built adversarial evals around common GTM misdiagnoses. The first version obeyed surface framing. The second overcorrected. The final version added counter-signals so routing became discriminating instead of blindly overriding.”

The key phrase: **failure-mode hardening**.

Show one small table:

- Case
- Expected route
- v1 result
- v2 result
- v3 result
- What changed

Do not dwell on 22/25. Dwell on “all 5 routing decisions correct” because routing is the core system behavior.

**5. Hormozi Angle**

Hormozi helps the demo because the Constraint Hierarchy is simple, teachable, and creates a clear routing structure.

It hurts credibility if you make it sound like “Hormozi wisdom bot for SaaS.” That feels unserious to operators.

Make the framework less cultish:

- Say “I used Hormozi’s hierarchy as the diagnostic taxonomy.”
- Don’t over-index on his name.
- Don’t show too many quotes.
- Emphasize the operational behavior: constraint diagnosis, routing, specialist brief, stress test.

The framework is not truly load-bearing. The load-bearing part is: **surface symptom vs root constraint diagnosis**.

**6. What’s Memorable**

Current memory: “Hormozi multi-agent thing.”

Better memory:  
**“The GTM agent that refuses the obvious answer and routes to the real constraint.”**

That is the sticky line.

In six months, they will not remember 7 agents, 88 source chunks, or 25 eval rules. They may remember: “He built the one that didn’t just answer the user. It challenged the premise.”

**7. What To Cut**

Cut:

- Deep knowledge-base explanation.
- Most of the architecture diagram.
- Number of source rows.
- Long explanation of all 7 agents.
- Detailed Hormozi background.
- Anything about H2 chunking unless asked.

Keep:

- The live diagnosis.
- The rejected surface answer.
- The specialist output.
- One eval table.
- One trace showing Triage → Offer Specialist → Synthesizer.

If you need to cut 2 minutes, kill the architecture tour. Show only enough architecture to prove it is not one prompt.

**8. Blind Spot**

You are overestimating how much “multi-agent” matters.

The judge cares whether the system does something a normal chatbot would not reliably do. The strongest behavior is disciplined misdiagnosis prevention. The weakest behavior is the agent count.

Second blind spot: your demo case is too perfect. It is engineered to land on Offer. A tough reviewer may think you built a maze where you know the exit.

Add one sentence in the Loom:

“I also tested cases where the system should stay with Sales, Pricing, Leads, or Retention, because otherwise this would just be an Offer hammer.”

That protects you from the obvious criticism.
